<h2>Description</h2>
<p>Perl officially stands for Practical Extraction and Report Language, except when it doesn&#8217;t. </p>
<p> Perl was originally a language optimized for scanning arbitrary text files, extracting information from those text files, and printing reports based on that information. It quickly became a good language for many system management tasks. Over the years, Perl has grown into a general-purpose programming language. It&#8217;s widely used for everything from quick &#8220;one-liners&#8221; to full-scale application development. </p>
<p> The language is intended to be practical (easy to use, efficient, complete) rather than beautiful (tiny, elegant, minimal). It combines (in the author&#8217;s opinion, anyway) some of the best features of <code>sed</code>, <code>awk</code>, and <code>sh</code>, making it familiar and easy to use for Unix users to whip up quick solutions to annoying problems. Its general-purpose programming facilities support procedural, functional, and object-oriented programming paradigms, making Perl a comfortable language for the long haul on major projects, whatever your bent. </p>
<p> Perl&#8217;s roots in text processing haven&#8217;t been forgotten over the years. It still boasts some of the most powerful regular expressions to be found anywhere, and its support for Unicode text is world-class. It handles all kinds of structured text, too, through an extensive collection of extensions. Those libraries, collected in the <font SIZE="-1">CPAN,</font> provide ready-made solutions to an astounding array of problems. When they haven&#8217;t set the standard themselves, they steal from the best &#8212; just like Perl itself.</p>

<h2>Diagnostics</h2>
<p>Using the <tt>&quot;use strict&quot;</tt> pragma ensures that all variables are properly declared and prevents other misuses of legacy Perl features. </p>
<p> The <tt>&quot;use warnings&quot;</tt> pragma produces some lovely diagnostics. One can also use the <code>-w</code> flag, but its use is normally discouraged, because it gets applied to all executed Perl code, including that not under your control. </p>
<p> See perldiag for explanations of all Perl&#8217;s diagnostics. The <tt>&quot;use diagnostics&quot;</tt> pragma automatically turns Perl&#8217;s normally terse warnings and errors into these longer forms. </p>
<p> Compilation errors will tell you the line number of the error, with an indication of the next token or token type that was to be examined. (In a script passed to Perl via <code>-e</code> switches, each <code>-e</code> is counted as one line.) </p>
<p> Setuid scripts have additional constraints that can produce error messages such as &#8220;Insecure dependency&#8221;. See perlsec. </p>
<p> Did we mention that you should definitely consider using the <code>use warnings</code> pragma?</p>
<h2>Bugs</h2>
<p>The behavior implied by the <code>use warnings</code> pragma is not mandatory. </p>
<p> Perl is at the mercy of your machine&#8217;s definitions of various operations such as type casting, <code>atof()</code>, and floating-point output with <code>sprintf()</code>. </p>
<p> If your stdio requires a seek or eof between reads and writes on a particular stream, so does Perl. (This doesn&#8217;t apply to <code>sysread()</code> and <code>syswrite()</code>.) </p>
<p> While none of the built-in data types have any arbitrary size limits (apart from memory size), there are still a few arbitrary limits: a given variable name may not be longer than 251 characters. Line numbers displayed by diagnostics are internally stored as short integers, so they are limited to a maximum of 65535 (higher numbers usually being affected by wraparound). </p>
<p> You may submit your bug reports (be sure to include full configuration information as output by the myconfig program in the perl source tree, or by <tt>&quot;perl -V&quot;</tt>) to &lt;<a rel="nofollow" href="https://github.com/Perl/perl5/issues">https://github.com/Perl/perl5/issues</a>&gt;. </p>
